Internet browsers are the most-used software apps on computers today.

## Different Browsers

"Browsers" today refer to internet browsers:

   * Google Chrome = https://developer.chrome.com/extensions/overview
   * Edge (from Microsoft) accepts extensions from other browsers
   * Firefox from Mozilla
   * Safari from Apple
   * Brave (into crypto currencies)
   * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.torproject.org/download/">Tor (The Onion Browser)</a> to "Protect yourself against tracking, surveillance, and censorship."
   <br /><br />

Contrast that with "Finder" used to find files and folders locally on your Apple macOS computer.

## Install browsers

The browsers themselves are installed by mac-setup.zsh script.

To enable invocation of the browser GUI from the CLI (command line Terminal), they are defined in <strong>aliases.zsh</strong>.

PROTIP: Install Tor on a cloud instance. The Tor browser was created by US intelligence. But they found out that adversaries were able to trace users' IP addresses, even when using VPNs by Tor and others. So Tor was open-sourced.

## Safe browsing

There are many websites setup by malicious people to steal your identity, money, and secrets.

*   Here's how to protect yourself: 

    ### Test

1.  [_] See if your email address appears on the "dark web" from data extracted from a website you gave your email to. 

    <a target="_blank" href="https://HaveIbeenPwned.com/">HaveIbeenPwned.com</a>

    Hackers use leaked emails to try to log into your other accounts and to send you phishing emails and bogus texts in your phone.

    PROTIP: With so many breaches, it's inevitable that your email address will eventually appear on the dark web. So do what's on this page to protect yourself.

2.  [_] Almost anyone can be uniquely identified with just an age, zip code. See if your (and your family members') age, address, and phone number appear in data broker websites:

    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.whitepages.com/">WhitePages.com</a> 
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.spokeo.com/">Spokeo.com</a> to 
    <br /><br />

    There are dozens of "data brokers" offering information for sale. They are legally compelled to remove your information if you ask. However, new ones crop up frequently. 

3.  [_] Get a subscription for a service to do it for you, automatically:
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.privacyduck.com/">PrivacyDuck.com</a>.
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.optoutprescreen.com/">OptOutPrescreen.com</a> 
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.dmachoice.org/">DMAchoice.org</a>
    <br /><br />


    ### You don't have to do what they ask

4.  [_] Resist giving out your phone number on web forms. When required, give them Jenny's number "310.867-5304". That way, not only you won't get spam calls and texts, you don't have to worry about someone using the same phone number you use to reset your passwords.

5.  [_] To follow-up with people you meet, lookup the other person's LinkedIn.com profile and connect with them for instant messaging on LinkedIn. That way, you can block them later if needed. More importantly, LinkedIn has a way to prove that they are who they say they are, through their verification of work email addresses and government IDs, such as on my profile:

    <a target="_blank" href="https://linkedin.com/in/wilsonmar">https://linkedin.com/in/wilsonmar</a>

    Check a potential connection on LinkedIn to avoid profiles created recently. Many connection requests are fake or from people who just want to sell you something.

6.  [_] Do not post your email address on profiles on LinkedIn, GitHub, etc. to be "harvested" by hackers.

7.  [_] Don't trust reviews on the company's own website. Research companies on known-good websites:

    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.glassdoor.com/">glassdoor.com</a> of reviews by  employees
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.bbb.org/">BBB.org</a>
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.trustpilot.com/">TrustPilot.com</a>
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.yelp.com/">yelp.com</a> of reviews by customers (and competitors)
    * <a target="_blank" href="https://www.twitter.com/">twitter.com/x.com</a> posts
    * Courthouse of lawsuits against the company
    <br /><br />

8.  [_] Be weary of communications with people you don't know, especially if a public email such as gmail.com, outlook.com, etc.  

    Be especially weary if urgency is expressed, meant to make you not take the time to verify their identity.

    ### Setup accounts safely

9.  [_] Set your email to <strong>not display images unless you click</strong>. Spammers use image URLs to tell whether an email address is valid. If you click on an image or link, they know it's valid. Malware can be installed when you click on a link or an image.

10. [_] <strong>Setup a different email only for banking</strong>, credit cards, and other financials. That way, if your regular email is compromised, hackers won't be able to reset your bank account password.

11. [_] Apply for <strong>credit cards and phone plans using a different zip code</strong> than your home address because your zip code is an item of verification, such as when you use your credit card at gas stations.

    BONUS TIP: Apply for a credit card (with a low limit such as $1,500) that you use in case your wallet is lost or stolen. That way, you won't have to wait for a replacement card to arrive in the mail. So keep that card in your suitcase zipper within an RFID-safe sleeve.

    BONUS TIP: Charge something on that card occasionally so you don't get canceled.

    BONUS TIP: In your suitcase and in your backpack, keep a list of phone numbers and companies to call when you lose your wallet. Don't put expiration dates on that card.

12. [_] Gmail has a feature to add a "+" and a word to your email address. For example, if your email address is "johndoe@gmail.com", you can provide "johndoe+341@gmail.com" to a website. That way, you can tell if they sold your email address to spammers. Keep track of your aliases in your password manager.

    Gmail also has strong spam filtering. But Gmail does sell your email address and contents to advertisers. So use a different email address for websites that you don't trust.

13.  [_] Provide fake answers to personal questions, and store them in your password vault. That way, hackers won't be able to reset your password by obtaining answers to those questions from social media and geneology websites.

14. [_] <strong>Install a password manager app</strong> that stores your passwords and other secrets in an encrypted vault. That way, it can tell you which password was used more than once. That way, if one of your passwords is compromised, hackers won't be able to use it to log into your other accounts.

    It also generates new unique ("strong") passwords for you. That way, you won't be tempted to use the same password for multiple websites, which is one of the most common ways people get hacked.

15. [_] Use your password manager to generate strong passwords for you. That way, you don't have to remember them. And you won't be tempted to use the same password for multiple websites, which is one of the most common ways people get hacked.

    PROTIP: <a target="_blank" href="https://1password.com/">1Password 7</a> which can store secrets locally. The "1" in "1Password" is because you only need to remember one password to unlock it. It's available for Mac, Windows, iOS, and Android. Note that 1Password also assigns another code used to recover your vault if you forget your password.

16. [_] Provide 1Password credentials to your "digital executor" -- someone you trust (such as a lawyer) to take care of your digital assets when you die or are incapacitated. That way, your family won't have to deal with the hassle of figuring out how to get access to your accounts when needed.

17. [_] Install <strong>Authy</strong> on your mobile phone to generate one-time codes for MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication). It's also available as a Chrome extension. It works like Google Authenticator. One unique feature is its data can be backed up so you can use it on multiple devices.

    With MFA, when your password is compromised, hackers still won't be able to log into your account.

18. [_] Enable MFA on all your important accounts, such as email, banking, credit cards, etc. It does take an extra bit of time, like opening doors for people who visit instead of leaving doors open for anyone to walk in. 


    ### Regular Habits for security

19. [_] In public (in bars, etc.), be aware of people and cameras behind you when you type in your password or PIN. Criminals who know your PIN have been known to grab or steal your phone so they can obtain your banking info for making withdrawls before you have time to change your password on a friend's phone.

20. [_] Make checking important accounts a part of your <strong>daily routine</strong>, along with something you do every day, such as making coffee, taking medicines, etc. That way, if your account is compromised, you'll be able to promptly take action.

21. [_] Make changing passwords on important websites part of your <strong>monthly routine</strong>. That way, if your password is compromised, hackers won't be able to use it for long.

22. [_] To make it easier to get everything back when you lose your laptop, buy an <strong>external USB hard drive to backup</strong> a full copy of your laptop and phone. Backups allow you to "Time Travel" to any time in the past when particular files were not "corrupted".

    PROTIP: If you're using a Mac, use <strong>Apple's Time Machine</strong> app to backup your computer to an external hard drive. If you're using Windows, use "File History" to backup.

23. [_] While scheduling appointments when you'll be leaving your house, also schedule time before you leave to take a backup. That way, if your computer is stolen, you won't miss any gaps in changes made.

24. [_] Setup your computer to ALSO backup automatically to the cloud. That way, if your computer and external hard drive are stolen, you'll still have a backup.

25. [_] Resist clicking links in emails and SMS texts that take you to a fake website, <strong>go to the website directly</strong>, using a web address you know is correct because you <strong>save a bookmark</strong> to each of the websites you frequent. 

    ### Browser extensions for security

26. [_] Instead of clicking on links in emails, right-click to copy the URL (web address) to see what various investigators say about it on Google's website stored among your browser bookmarks:

    <a target="_blank" href="https://www.virustotal.com/gui/home/url">https://www.virustotal.com/gui/home/url</a>

    Virus Total is a free service (from Google) that aggregates various reports of viruses, worms, trojans, etc. found in  websites and files. It also scans files.

27. [_] To have URLs checked automatically when you click, install Google's <a target="_blank" href="https://chrome.google.com/webstore/detail/google-safe-browsing/bkdgflcldnnnapblkhphbgpggdiikppg">Google Safe Browsing extension for Chrome</a>. 

    It warns you if a website you're trying to visit is suspected of phishing or malware.

28. [_] Install a VPN (Virtual Private Network) app to encrypt your internet traffic. That way, when you're using a public WiFi network (such as at Starbucks, airports, etc.), hackers can't intercept your traffic and steal your secrets.

    Popular VPN service available for Mac, Windows, iOS, and Android include NordVPN and <a target="_blank" href="https://www.expressvpn.com/">ExpressVPN</a>. Beware that some VPN services are scams that capture everything you type and do, so research before choosing one.

29. [_] Install a <strong>browser extension</strong> that blocks ads and trackers. That way, you won't be tracked by advertisers, and you won't be exposed to malicious ads. <a target="_blank" href="https://www.ghostery.com/">Ghostery</a> is a popular browser extension that's available for Chrome, Firefox, Safari, Opera, and Edge.

## Are extensions safe?

Browser extensions are wonderful because of their ability to <strong>inject JavaScript</strong> automation code within internet browsers that modify web content users see and interact with -- changing and extending the behavior of the browser itself.

That can include malicious actions such as theft of your login credentials and other secrets.


<a name="Profiles"></a>

## PROTIP: Use Browser Profiles

Websites (including Azure) stores your browser history, what account you logged in, etc. in "cookies" associated with your browser account.

That's how you get returned to the last account used when you go back to a website.

PROTIP: Setup <strong>different browser profiles</strong> on the same browser, associated with different profile avatars and colors: <strong>one for each account (email)</strong>.

1. Click your browser's avatar picture at the upper-right corner:

   * The <a href="#MSAccount">Learn account</a> using your personal email (such as at gmail.com).
   * The account associated with your Visual Studio benefit (using your work email)
   * Each of your work accounts (to do your job as an Administrator).
   <br /><br />

1. Do the above for each browser (Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge, Firefox, etc.).


## Can the browser:

   * detect and block access to suspicious websites, downloads and extensions
   * block access to known malicious websites (identified by virustotal.com)
   * control access to specific company web pages and applications
   * block website access to the user's camera and microphone
   * recognize websites that offer multi-factor user authentication, then enforce its use

An administrator can:
   * view browser settings, versions and installed extensions
   * manage browsers on mobile devices
   * restrict end-user control of settings
   * force-install browser extensions
   * control which extensions browser users install
   * audit user activity logs

Such administration controls (such as JAMF) can be centralized via policies that:
   * are administered from a cloud interface
   * are integrated with other security tools
   * are applied to the browser across operating systems
   * are administered using an on-premises framework

        pageUrl: {hostEquals: 'developer.chrome.com'},
      })],
      actions: [new chrome.declarativeContent.ShowPageAction()]
    }]);
  });
});

To listen for clicks on the browser action, pass a message (URL) to a background script.

<pre>"background": {
  "scripts": ["background.js"]
}</pre>

background.js has access to every Chrome API but cannot access the current page. As Google puts it:

<pre>// background.js
&nbsp;
// Called when the user clicks on the browser action.
chrome.browserAction.onClicked.addListener(function(tab) {
  // Send a message to the active tab
  chrome.tabs.query({active: true, currentWindow: true}, function(tabs) {
    var activeTab = tabs[0];
    chrome.tabs.sendMessage(activeTab.id, {"message": "clicked_browser_action"});
  });
});</pre>
