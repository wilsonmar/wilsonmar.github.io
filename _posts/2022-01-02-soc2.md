SOC stands for (“Service Organization Control") reports as defined by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA).<a href="#[1]">[1]</a> 

The AICPA was formed as an association of <a target="_blank" href="https://insidepublicaccounting.com/top-firms/ipa-400/">independent CPA firms</a> (such as PwC, Deloitte, EY, KPMG, etc.) who are approved by a company's shareholders to perform internal audits. SOC auditing is another line of business, but for a different firm.

The report that customers request from service providers is a <strong>SOC2 Type II report each year</strong> from a CPA firm hired by each service provider to evaluate and attest that there is proof the service provider indeed has measures in place to protect the integrity, confidentiality, and privacy of data.

## Types of SOC

The "2" in "SOC2" and "Type II" refers to the specific type of report issued.

PROTIP: Service providers are regularly advised to limit their first SOC 2 audit to just Security and only include additional criteria if necessary.

<strong>Type 1 reports</strong> is on audits of a service organization’s controls over <strong>financial reporting standards</strong>. It is applicable only to service organizations which perform <strong>outsourced services</strong> that affect the financial statements of another Company (the "User Organization"), such as
Payroll Processing, Loan Servicing, Data Center/Co-Location/Network Monitoring Services, Software as a Service (SaaS), Medical Claims Processors, etc.

NOTE: "POLICIES" refer to rules defined to protect assets.
"CONTROLS" are rules are implemented (such as use MFA, etc.).

Type 2 reports are provided to prospective clients and other qualified parties.

Type 3 reports are a simplified version of the SOC 2 report. It is designed to <strong>publicly</strong> attest that the service provider has completed a SOC 2 assessment, while also limiting the information to what is relevant to public parties. SOC 3 report were created as a result of the growing demand for a public facing report.

Also, there are two report types:

SOC Type I reports address the suitability of policies and procedures in operation at a <strong>specific moment in time</strong>.

"SOC Type II" reports address both the suitability and <strong>effectiveness</strong> of policies and procedures over a <strong>period of time</strong> -- no less than six months (usually a year). Since this report takes into account historical processes, it is a more accurate and comprehensive audit. However, many companies are not able to adequately generate data as the basis for an audit until they have adequate controls in place.

These defined controls are a series of standards designed to help measure how well a given service organization conducts and regulates its information. They are designed to provide clients confidence that an organization can be trusted to keep their data secure.


## SSAE 18

The AICPA "Statement on Standards for Attestation Engagements" (SSAE) define standards auditors use to conduct audits. Verion 16 of SSAE replaced SAS ("Statement on Auditing Standards") 70 on 2011. <a target="_blank" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSAE_No._18">SSAE version 18</a> <a target="_blank" href="https://us.aicpa.org/content/dam/aicpa/research/standards/auditattest/downloadabledocuments/ssae-no-18.pdf">PDF</a> was created May 2017. Its requirements defines some acronymns:
<a name="IPE"></a>
* <strong>IPE</strong> (Information Produced by the Entity): Companies must provide evidence of the accuracy of any information provided. Examples include SQL queries or Tableau report parameters.

* Vendor management and monitoring of <strong>sub-service organizations</strong>: Service providers or data centers must include controls for sub-service organizations. The goal is to ensure that anybody with access to the data is adhering to control standards.

* <strong>CUECs</strong> or Complementary User Entity Controls: limited to controls that are needed to achieve the stated control objectives

* Internal audit and regulatory examinations: service organizations read the latest reports relating to internal and regulatory examinations. For example, SOC Cybersecurity examination and updated trust services principles went into effect on December 15th, 2018.

The equivalent for SSAE 18 internationally is the ISAE 3402 (International Standards for Audit Engagements) publisehd by the International Auditing and Standards Board.

1. Define the <strong>operating goals</strong> of your audit. You should ask yourself what your clients are most likely to want to know. You know the parameters of the SOC 2 audit. If financial information is  handled, a SOC 1 audit is needed as well.

1. Define the <strong>scope</strong> of the SOC 2 audit. They typically address infrastructure, software, data, risk management,  procedures, and people. Decide which trust principles to include. Any TSC added increases the scope of the  audit. Again, choose the TSCs that are most likely to concern your clients.

1. Identify and address <strong>regulatory and compliance requirements</strong>.

1. Review and <strong>write security policies and procedures</strong> which the auditor will examine. Ensure that everyone follow them. A system and training may be needed to organize and format what is being written.

1. <strong>Create data</strong> as the basis for audit and reporting during the <strong>audit period</strong>. 

1. Perform a <strong>readiness assessment</strong>, like a "dress rehearsal" in the theater, to identify issues in audit preparations. You can use an internal auditor or hire an auditing firm to guide you. This often occur after pen-testing.

1. Evaluate and <strong>hire a certified external auditor</strong> (with experience in your particular industry). The auditor will:

   * work with you to choose agreed-upon testing dates
   * Give you a list of required documentation in advance of the audit
   * Visit your site for document reviews, employee interviews, and walk-throughs

## Type 2 trust 

Type 2 reports focus on five <strong>trust principals</strong> which each service organization deem relevant to their business <a href="#[3]">[3]</a>:

<a target="_blank" href="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/300046/147965363-8ba744e4-5b0c-4799-b929-0b26e12ceaac.jpg">
<img alt="soc2-tsc-from-dash-1300x712" src="https://user-images.githubusercontent.com/300046/147965363-8ba744e4-5b0c-4799-b929-0b26e12ceaac.jpg"></a>

   * <strong>Security</strong> – Information and system assets are protected against unauthorized access, unauthorized disclosure of information and damage to systems that could compromise security availability confidentiality, integrity, and privacy of data or systems and affect the entity’s ability to meet its objectives.

   * <strong>Availability</strong> – Information and organizational systems are available (accessible) for operation and use to meet the entity’s objective requirements. Controls include fail-over.

   * <strong>Integrity</strong> – System processing is complete, valid, accurate, timely, and authorized to meet the entity’s objectives.

   * <strong>Confidentiality</strong> – Information designated as confidential is protected (such as passwords, encryption using security certificates, etc.) to meet the entity’s objectives.

   * <strong>Privacy</strong> – Personal information is collected, used, retained, disclosed and disposed of to meet the entity’s objectives.

The categories above all share a set of trust services criteria known as the <a href="#StandardCriteria">standard criteria</a> for each trust principal.

Additionally, each TSC contains <strong>points of focus</strong>  to assist management when designing, implementing,
and operating controls.

<a name="StandardCriteria"></a>

## Trust Standard Criteria

The Trust Service Criteria (<strong>TSC</strong>) for each trust principal include these Standard Criteria:

* Control environment
* Communication and information
* Risk assessment
* Monitoring activities
* Control activities – which are further broken out by:
   - Logical and physical access
   - System Operational Effectiveness
   - Change Management
   - Risk Mitigation
<br /><br />

Each of the above criteria are addressed in every SOC audit. 
Additionally, there may be more TSC’s which needed to be evaluated in addition to the standard criteria, depending on which TSC categories are being assessed.

Since 2017, a <strong>SOC 2+</strong> report which allow the services organization to address additional criteria from other compliance standards such as HITECH, HIPAA compliance, ISO 27001, Cloud Security Alliance (CSA), NIST 800-53 or COBIT 5.

When you order your compliance audit, you can decide which TSC categories are the most important. Base your decisions on what clients are most likely to want. Doing so will ensure that clients get the information they need. They will be less likely to come back to you with questions if they are addressed in the SOC 2 report.
